The Clock Starts When the Notice Drops

Every recall in the United States becomes public the moment it is filed. The FDA, USDA, and CPSC publish them as structured data: company name, product, quantity, how far it spread, how dangerous it is. Anyone can read it. Almost nobody uses it the way it should be used.

Most people treat a recall as news. It is not news. It is a timestamp on a company entering the most acute operational crisis it will face all year. The day that notice publishes, someone inside that company is being told to find warehousing, destruction, and paperwork immediately, and they have no idea who to call.

The classification tells you how hot it is. A Class I recall means the product can seriously hurt or kill someone, which means maximum urgency and maximum willingness to pay for competence. The quantity tells you the scale of the physical problem. The distribution pattern tells you whether it is one state or thirty.

A recall isn’t a headline. It’s a company announcing, in public, that it needs help this week.

Read that way, the timing stops being guesswork. You are not cold-calling and hoping someone happens to have a problem. You are reaching the exact people who just got the exact bad news, inside the narrow window where they will actually answer.

The operators who win recall work are not the ones with the best website. They are the ones already positioned when the notice drops. Our job is to be positioned for them: to watch the filings, and put the right warehouse in front of the right crisis before the window closes.
